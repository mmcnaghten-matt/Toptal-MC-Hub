What It Is

Framework Overview

The NIST AI Risk Management Framework (AI RMF 1.0), published January 2023, is the leading voluntary standard for managing AI risks in the United States. It is sector-agnostic, scalable to any organization size, and increasingly referenced in enterprise procurement, federal contracts, and state-level AI regulation. The framework does not mandate specific tools or technologies — it defines outcomes and leaves implementation to the organization.

Function 1 · Cross-cutting
GOVERN
6 categories · ~20 subcategories

Establishes organizational culture, policies, and accountability. Informs and enables all other functions — should be in place first and maintained throughout.

  • AI use policies & risk thresholds
  • Roles, accountability, executive sponsorship
  • Cross-functional AI governance committee
  • Workforce training & culture
  • Third-party / vendor AI oversight
Function 2 · Pre-deployment
MAP
5 categories · 18 subcategories

Establishes context and identifies risks before deployment. Foreseeable misuse must be considered alongside intended use.

  • Use case documentation & data provenance
  • Stakeholder impact assessment
  • Harm identification (physical, financial, reputational)
  • Risk identification across AI lifecycle
  • Known limitations & failure mode documentation
Function 3 · Assess & Monitor
MEASURE
5 categories · 22 subcategories

Quantifies risks through testing, metrics, and ongoing monitoring. Translates identified risks into measurable signals.

  • Testing & evaluation (including red-teaming)
  • Bias & fairness assessment
  • Performance benchmarking
  • Continuous monitoring & drift detection
  • Hallucination rate measurement
Function 4 · Act on Risks
MANAGE
3 categories · ~12 subcategories

Prioritizes and addresses risks through treatment, mitigation, and response planning. Translates measurement into action.

  • Risk prioritization & scoring
  • Risk treatment: avoid / mitigate / transfer / accept
  • Control implementation
  • Incident response & rollback procedures
  • Post-incident review & control updates
The key structural insight: GOVERN applies to all AI activity across the entire organization. MAP, MEASURE, and MANAGE apply at the individual AI system level, at each stage of the system's lifecycle. You set up governance once (at the organization level) and run MAP → MEASURE → MANAGE for each AI system or use case.
Function 1 · Foundational

GOVERN — Establish Culture & Accountability

GOVERN is the only function that applies across the entire organization rather than system-by-system. Without it, MAP/MEASURE/MANAGE lack context, authority, and ownership. Primary ownership typically sits with the CIO, CISO, General Counsel, or Chief Risk Officer.

GV.1 Organizational Policies & Risk Thresholds

Define what AI the organization will and won't do, at what risk tolerance, and with what approval process. Policies must be documented, version-controlled, and aligned to legal obligations.

AI use policy document Risk tolerance thresholds by use case Approval workflows for high-risk AI Incident response procedures Version history & audit trail
GV.2 Roles, Responsibilities & Accountability

Clear ownership is the single most cited gap in AI governance failures. Define who is accountable for each AI system, who approves deployment, and who is responsible when things go wrong.

Executive AI sponsor (CIO / CRO) Model Owner (BU leader — risk & performance) AI Champion (BU operational role) RACI matrix for AI risk process Cross-functional governance committee Committee charters & meeting cadence
GV.3 Workforce Training & Culture

Technical controls reduce harm; culture prevents it. Organizations that treat governance as a training and culture challenge — not just a policy exercise — consistently outperform those that don't.

AI risk awareness training (role-based) AI literacy program — all staff Safe reporting channels (no-retaliation) Training completion tracking
GV.4 Third-Party & Vendor AI Oversight

Most enterprises deploy more vendor-built AI than internally-built AI. Governance that stops at the organization's boundary leaves the majority of AI risk unaddressed.

AI security questionnaires for vendors AI governance requirements in contracts Periodic third-party compliance audits Vendor registry (same as agent registry)
GV.5 Policies for AI Risk Management (Organizational)

Extend AI governance into broader enterprise risk management programs — AI risk should not be siloed from cyber risk, operational risk, or compliance risk.

AI risk integrated into ERM program Board-level AI risk reporting AI risk appetite statement Alignment to ISO 42001 / EU AI Act where applicable
GV.6 Policies for AI Risk Management (Workforce)

Policies governing how employees interact with, build, and deploy AI — including sanctioned use, prohibited use, and citizen development.

Approved AI tools catalog Prohibited AI uses (clearly defined) Citizen development policy & guardrails Shadow AI disclosure / amnesty program
Function 2 · Pre-deployment Context

MAP — Understand Context & Identify Risks

MAP happens before deployment. Its job is to understand the operating environment, identify who could be affected and how, and document what could go wrong — including ways the system might be misused. MAP is where risk classification occurs.

MP.1 Context & Use Case Analysis

Document the intended purpose, user populations, and operating environment for every AI system. Include foreseeable misuse — not just intended use.

Use case definition with user populations Foreseeable misuse documentation Deployment environment & integration points Data sources, quality, & provenance Architecture diagrams
MP.2 Stakeholder Impact Assessment

Identify everyone affected by AI decisions — including indirect stakeholders who may not interact with the system directly. Assess potential harms across harm categories.

Stakeholder map (direct & indirect) Harm assessment: physical, psychological, financial, reputational Disparate impact analysis (protected classes) Community engagement records (where applicable)
MP.3 Risk Identification Across the AI Lifecycle

Systematically identify risks at each stage: training data, model design, deployment, integration, and ongoing operation. Document known limitations and failure modes before deployment.

Risk register (per system) Risks cataloged against all 7 trustworthy AI characteristics Training data risk assessment Known limitations documented (model card) Failure mode & effects analysis
MP.4 Risk Classification & Prioritization

Classify each AI system by risk tier. Risk classification determines approval pathway, monitoring intensity, and documentation requirements. Calibrate approval to risk — not all AI should face the same governance overhead.

Risk tier classification (Low / Medium / High) Risk scoring criteria (documented) Approval pathway by tier Escalation triggers
MP.5 Scientific & Technical Risk Understanding

Ensure decision-makers understand the technical risks inherent in the AI approach being used — including model type, training methodology, and known limitations of the underlying technology.

Technical risk summary (for non-technical stakeholders) Model card / system card Limitations documented in plain language
Function 3 · Assess & Quantify

MEASURE — Test, Benchmark & Monitor

MEASURE translates identified risks into quantifiable signals. It covers pre-deployment testing, bias assessment, and the ongoing monitoring required to detect performance degradation, drift, and emerging risks. Measurement is continuous — not a one-time exercise.

MS.1 AI Testing & Evaluation

Rigorous testing before and after deployment. Testing scope should match risk level — high-risk applications warrant adversarial testing and red-teaming.

Performance testing across diverse conditions Edge case & adversarial testing Prompt injection & jailbreak testing (GenAI) Red team exercises (high-risk) Hallucination rate benchmarking Benchmark scores (documented)
MS.2 Bias & Fairness Assessment

Measure and document bias across protected categories before deployment and on a defined ongoing cadence. Fairness metrics must be defined for the specific use case — no universal metric applies.

Use-case-specific fairness metrics (defined upfront) Disparate impact testing by demographic group Training & evaluation data representation analysis Bias assessment report (versioned)
MS.3 Continuous Monitoring

Establish production monitoring to detect performance degradation, data drift, and behavioral anomalies. Monitoring should generate evidence automatically — not be assembled at audit time.

Production performance metrics dashboard Data drift & distribution shift detection Anomalous output alerting Behavioral monitoring (agents) Automated compliance reporting Incident log & drift reports
MS.4 Trustworthy AI Characteristics Assessment

Assess system performance against all seven trustworthy AI characteristics (see below) on a defined review cycle. Document gaps and remediation plans.

Assessment against 7 trustworthy AI criteria Documented gaps & remediation plans Defined review cycle (e.g., annual for low-risk, quarterly for high)
MS.5 Explainability & Interpretability

Ensure that AI outputs can be explained to the relevant stakeholders — the level of explainability required depends on the decision context and who is affected.

Explainability approach documented per use case Human-readable output explanations (where required) Interpretability appropriate to decision stakes
Function 4 · Act on Risks

MANAGE — Treat, Mitigate & Respond

MANAGE is where measurement becomes action. It covers risk prioritization, treatment selection, control implementation, and incident response. The framework identifies four treatment options: avoid, mitigate, transfer, or accept.

MG.1 Risk Prioritization

Score and prioritize risks using consistent criteria across all AI systems, aligned to the broader enterprise risk framework. Consider reversibility — risks that are hard to undo warrant greater caution.

Risk scoring matrix (likelihood × impact) Reversibility & remediation difficulty factor Regulatory & reputational implications weighting Prioritized risk register (reviewed regularly)
MG.2 Risk Treatment Options

For each identified risk, select one of four treatment strategies. The treatment decision must be documented with executive sign-off for "Accept" decisions on high-risk items.

Avoid — don't deploy this use case Mitigate — implement controls to reduce to acceptable level Transfer — contracts, insurance, shared responsibility Accept — document with executive approval (high-risk requires sign-off) Treatment decisions documented & reviewable
MG.3 Incident Response & Recovery

Prepare for AI failures before they happen. Rollback mechanisms, human takeover protocols, and post-incident reviews are the minimum viable incident response capability for any AI system in production.

AI-specific incident response plan Rollback & human takeover mechanisms Escalation contacts & decision authority Post-incident review process Control update procedures post-incident Incident logs retained for audit
Framework Foundation

Seven Characteristics of Trustworthy AI

The NIST AI RMF is organized around seven characteristics that AI systems should exhibit to be considered trustworthy. Risk assessment across MAP and MEASURE is evaluated against all seven. An AI system that fails on any one characteristic is not fully trustworthy.

✓
Valid & Reliable
Consistent, accurate performance within defined parameters across conditions and over time. The system does what it claims to do.
🛡
Safe
No unreasonable harm to people or the environment — including physical, psychological, financial, and societal harm. Safety failures can be irreversible.
🔒
Secure & Resilient
Protected from adversarial attacks (including prompt injection), able to maintain function under stress, and recoverable from disruption. Includes data integrity.
👁
Accountable & Transparent
Clear ownership of outcomes, visible decision processes, and audit trails. Someone is responsible for what this system does — and can be found.
💡
Explainable & Interpretable
Outputs can be understood by relevant stakeholders. The level of explainability required scales with the stakes of the decision and who is affected.
🔐
Privacy-Enhanced
Individual privacy is protected throughout the AI lifecycle — data collection, training, deployment, and output. Includes de-identification and data minimization.
⚖
Fair — Bias Managed
Regular auditing for discriminatory outputs with corrective action. Fairness must be defined per use case — no single metric applies universally.
NIST AI 600-1 · Released July 2024

Generative AI Profile — 12 Risk Categories

Released July 26, 2024 (per Executive Order 14110), the NIST AI 600-1 Generative AI Profile identifies 12 risks unique to or significantly exacerbated by generative AI and LLMs. It provides 200+ suggested actions mapped across GOVERN, MAP, MEASURE, and MANAGE. Organizations deploying LLMs or generative AI should layer this profile on top of AI RMF 1.0.

1
CBRN Information
Lowered barrier to accessing chemical, biological, radiological, and nuclear weapons information via AI-assisted research.
2
Confabulation
Generation of false or misleading content presented with false confidence — commonly called "hallucination." Ranges from 0.7% (best-in-class) to 25%+ in deployed models.
3
Dangerous Content
Creation of violent, hateful, or otherwise harmful content. Includes content that could incite real-world harm or facilitate harassment.
4
Data Privacy
Unauthorized exposure, leakage, or re-identification of personal data — through training data, model outputs, or retrieval-augmented generation.
5
Environmental Impacts
Significant energy consumption and carbon emissions from training and inference at scale. Increasingly a regulatory and ESG disclosure consideration.
6
Harmful Bias
Reinforcement or amplification of stereotypes and discriminatory outputs — particularly at the intersection of training data representation and deployment context.
7
Homogenization
Reduction in content diversity and perspectives as AI-generated content dominates. Risk of monoculture in knowledge creation and decision-making.
8
Information Integrity
Generation and amplification of misinformation and disinformation. Deepfakes and synthetic media are a significant sub-risk in this category.
9
Information Security
Lowered barrier to cybersecurity attacks — including code generation for exploits, spear-phishing at scale, and adversarial attacks on AI systems themselves.
10
Intellectual Property
Copyright concerns in both training data ingestion and generated outputs. Ongoing legal uncertainty — organizations should track jurisdiction-specific developments.
11
Obscene Content
Generation of sexual, violent, or otherwise illegal content. Includes non-consensual intimate images — a key regulatory focus in 2024–2026.
12
Value Chain & Component Risks
Risks inherited from third-party components — foundation models, fine-tuning datasets, plugins, and RAG data sources. Governance must extend to the supply chain.
Regulatory Alignment

Framework Crosswalk

NIST AI RMF is voluntary but widely adopted as a baseline. Organizations implementing it build a strong foundation for compliance with other frameworks and emerging regulations. The table below maps AI RMF functions to key regulatory and standards equivalents.

AI RMF Function EU AI Act ISO 42001 What It Means in Practice
GOVERN Quality Management System (Art. 17) · Transparency & Accountability obligations Clauses 5–7: Leadership, Planning, Support Policies, roles, and governance structures required by all three frameworks — implement once, satisfy all three.
MAP Risk Classification (Art. 6) · Prohibited AI (Art. 5) · Conformity assessment Clause 6.1: AI Risk Assessment MAP outputs (use case docs, stakeholder assessments, risk classification) directly satisfy EU Act conformity requirements for high-risk systems.
MEASURE Testing & Monitoring obligations (Art. 9) · Accuracy, robustness, cybersecurity requirements Clauses 9–10: Performance Evaluation, Improvement Testing records, bias reports, and monitoring logs produced in MEASURE become the evidence required for EU AI Act post-market surveillance.
MANAGE Risk Management System (Art. 9) · Incident reporting to authorities Clause 8: AI System Operation Incident response plans, treatment decisions, and post-incident reviews satisfy EU Act serious incident reporting and ISO 42001 operational control requirements.
Status as of June 2026: NIST AI RMF remains voluntary for US private-sector organizations. The Federal AI Risk Management Act (HR6936) would make it mandatory for federal agencies and contractors — status: proposed. Colorado's original 2024 AI Act offered a safe harbor for NIST AI RMF compliance, but that statute was repealed and replaced by SB 26-189 (effective Jan 1, 2027) which does not carry the safe harbor — the framework remains strongly recommended practice for meeting SB 26-189's documentation and disclosure duties, but is no longer a codified legal defense in Colorado.
